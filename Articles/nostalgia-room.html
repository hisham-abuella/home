Nostalgia Room: a 1999 Bedroom in the Browser

November 21, 2026 6 min read Hisham Abuella اقرا بالعربي

A 3D Egyptian bedroom from the 90s and early 2000s that you walk around in your browser: a TV with an antenna, a cassette player, dial-up internet, a cat. About eight hours of my work, over three days, with Claude Code.

Listen to this articleNarrated in my own voice

Some rooms you can still smell. A TV you had to hit on the side, a cassette you rewound with a pencil, an internet connection that dropped the moment someone picked up the phone. I wanted to put that room in a browser, so anyone who grew up in Egypt then can walk back into it.

It is called أوضة زمان (“the old room”), or Nostalgia Masry, and it is live at nostalgia-masry.com. I love creating UIs with Claude, and this one let me build a whole world instead of a screen.

The Inspiration

The idea is not mine. The 90s Room by Boared Games is a nostalgic room you walk through, and it stayed with me. It is credited in the project, and everything inside my room is my own: Egyptian, in Egyptian Arabic, with its own pictures, sounds and games.

The second source is memory. Before building anything I asked Claude to collect what an Egyptian kid of that time would recognise: the weekly kids’ magazines, the school bag with a clasp, the cyber café and MSN, the free dial-up numbers that began with 0777, Ramadan fawazir, a football final everyone watched. Every agent on the project judges its work by one question: would someone who lived 1990 to 2010 smile and say “yaaah”?

Three Days, About Eight Hours

I built it over three days in November 2026 and shipped version 1.2 on the third day. In my own time that was about eight hours. The project has 117 commits, and the rest of the typing was Claude Code and a team of subagents, each with one area: the frontend, the games, phones, security, hosting, rights and Islamic content.

Walking Around

The room is plain HTML and JavaScript with three.js, no framework and no build step. Every picture is made for the room, and the lighting does most of the work: a weak yellow bulb, the TV glow and dark corners, so it looks like a room you remember, not a showroom.

Play With Everything

I did not want a museum. The TV has a programme guide with embedded clips, the radio dial tunes real stations, and the family PC dials up and drops the line when someone lifts the phone. On the floor table there are games with computer opponents that have Egyptian names: a property-trading game of Arab countries called Bank El Haz, tarneeb, chess, tawla and more. There are PS2 discs, and Susu the cat sleeps on the bed and chases the ball.

The Rules I Set

A nostalgia project can easily slide into copying. So the rules are written down, and every agent loads them first:

  • Inspired by, never copied. Posters, logos and packaging in the room are invented. Real brands appear only as words in a memory.
  • Real songs, shows and matches open from their official sources. They are embeds or links, never copies.
  • Free-licensed media only, always credited.
  • Warm and family-friendly, with no real people’s faces in the generated pictures.

Safe and Private

Privacy and safety matter to me, so I built it that way from the start. As I write this, it has no accounts, no cookies and no ads, and your settings and progress stay in your browser. It only counts anonymous numbers, like how many times a game was opened, and it respects Do Not Track. Before each release a security review reads the code, and a gate blocks a push that breaks the rules, so I could move fast without being careless. This describes today’s version and may change as the room grows; the privacy page always has the current details.

What I Learned

Rules first, then speed. Writing the rules and the agents’ roles on day one is what let three days of fast changes stay consistent.

Details sell the feeling. An antenna that needs holding, a line that drops, a clock that chimes on the hour. Small, working things did more than detailed models.

Phones are the real audience. Most people will open it on a mid-range phone over mobile data, so the room is built to be light.

A room is not remembered by its furniture. It is remembered by what you did in it.

What’s Next

More objects and more memories, photos for the few cards still waiting for one, and, in the plan, other places beyond this room. If you grew up in Egypt then, go in and tell me what is missing.

Go In, and Help It Grow

Walk into the room at nostalgia-masry.com. It runs in any browser, on a phone or a laptop. If you enjoy it and want to help me keep adding memories, you can support it on Ko-fi. The payment happens on Ko-fi, and I never see your card details. You can also take a piece of the room home from the Nostalgia Masry shop on Etsy.
