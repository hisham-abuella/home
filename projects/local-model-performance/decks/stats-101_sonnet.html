Learning objectives for the module: explain what statistics is for and tell population from sample, parameter from statistic; classify variables and levels of measurement; describe how data are collected and spot bias; choose a graph; calculate and interpret mean, median, mode, range, IQR and standard deviation; find outliers with the 1.5 x IQR rule, compute z-scores and use the 68-95-99.7 rule; and explain why correlation does not imply causation.

Statistics is the science of collecting, describing and drawing conclusions from data.

Statistics works in the presence of variation and uncertainty: no two students have the same commute, and no sample is a perfect copy of the whole group. Three verbs to remember: collect, describe, conclude. Today is mostly the first two.

Takeaway

A huge biased sample is worse than a small well-chosen one

George Gallup, with a much smaller but better-chosen sample of about 50,000 people, correctly predicted Roosevelt's win. How data are collected matters more than how much there is.

First statement of our main idea: how the data are collected matters more than how much there is. Gallup used about 50,000 people and got it right; the Digest used millions and got it wrong. Keep this in mind: we will return to it at the bias slide and in the summary.

Where do the data come from?

A sample gives a statistic that estimates the population's parameter

Population: the entire group we want to know about, for example all students at the university. Sample: the part we actually collect data from, for example 200 students who answered a survey. A parameter is a number describing the population, usually unknown, written mu for the mean and sigma for the standard deviation. A statistic is computed from the sample to estimate the parameter: x-bar for the mean, s for the standard deviation. Memory aid: P with P, S with S. Click through the four steps.

What kind of data are they?

Every variable is categorical or numerical

Categorical nominal: categories with no order, such as blood type, favourite colour, country. Categorical ordinal: categories with a natural order, such as satisfaction low, medium, high, or year of study. Numerical discrete: counts, whole numbers, such as number of siblings or goals scored. Numerical continuous: measurements on a scale, such as height, time, temperature. Watch out: numbers are not always numerical data. A postcode or student ID is categorical, and averaging them means nothing.

Where do the data come from?

Only an experiment can show cause and effect

Observational study

Researchers observe and measure without assigning treatments, e.g. surveying students about sleep and grades. It can show an association, but not cause and effect.

Experiment

Researchers assign treatments and compare outcomes. Only a well-designed randomised experiment can establish cause and effect.

The key word is assign. In the sleep and grades survey, nobody assigned sleep, so students who sleep more may differ in many other ways. In an experiment the researchers assign the treatments.

Where do the data come from?

A good experiment has four ingredients

  1. Control group

    for comparison

  2. Random assignment

    groups alike apart from the treatment

  3. Placebo

    where possible

  4. Blinding

    double-blind: neither subjects nor researchers know who got what

A control group gives the comparison. Random assignment to groups means the groups are alike apart from the treatment. A placebo is used where possible, and blinding goes further: in a double-blind experiment neither the subjects nor the researchers know who got what.

Where do the data come from?

Random sampling methods beat convenience

MethodHow it worksExample

Four random methods and one to avoid. Simple random: every group of n individuals has the same chance of being chosen. Stratified: split into groups called strata and take a random sample from each. Cluster: split into groups and randomly choose whole groups. Systematic: choose every k-th individual from a list after a random start. Convenience: whoever is easiest to reach, not random, avoid.

Four ways a sample can mislead

  • Selection bias. Part of the population is left out.
  • Non-response bias. Non-responders differ from responders.
  • Response bias. Wording pushes people to an answer.
  • Voluntary response bias. Only strong opinions volunteer.

Bias is a systematic error that pushes results away from the truth in one direction. Selection bias: the Literary Digest's phone and car lists left out poorer voters in 1936. Response bias example wording: "Don't you agree that...?" Voluntary response: online polls and call-in shows.

A bigger sample does not fix bias. It only makes a biased answer look more precise.

Second statement of the main idea, in different words: collection beats quantity. This is the Literary Digest lesson in one line. Fence: bigger is not automatically better.

What do the data say?

Describe every distribution by shape, centre, spread and unusual features

Shape: symmetric, skewed right with a long tail to the right (for example incomes), or skewed left; one peak is unimodal, two peaks is bimodal. Then centre, spread, and unusual features such as outliers and gaps. The curves are schematic.

What do the data say? · Centre

Ten commute times: mean 26, median 21, mode 15

12, 15, 15, 18, 20, 22, 25, 27, 30, 76 minutes

26 minMean: sum 260 ÷ n = 10
21 minMedian: middle values 20 and 22
15 minMode: appears twice

Mean: add the values and divide by how many, x-bar = sum of x over n. Median: the middle value of the ordered data, the average of the two middle values if n is even. Mode: the most frequent value. Work it through: sum is 260, n is 10, so mean 26. The ordered middle values are 20 and 22, so median 21. The mode is 15 because it appears twice.

What do the data say? · Centre

One outlier drags the mean but barely moves the median

Remove the 76-minute commute: the mean drops from 26 to about 20.4, the median only from 21 to 20.

Commute example, 10 students

The median is resistant to outliers; the mean is not. In a right-skewed distribution the mean is pulled above the median; in a left-skewed one, below. Use the median for skewed data such as house prices and incomes, and the mean for roughly symmetric data.

What do the data say? · Spread

Range is fragile; IQR and SD tell the spread better

64 minRange: 76 − 12, very sensitive to outliers
12 minIQR: Q3 − Q1 = 27 − 15, the middle 50%
18.5 minStandard deviation s, about

Range is maximum minus minimum. Quartiles: Q1 is the median of the lower half, 12, 15, 15, 18, 20, so Q1 = 15; Q3 is the median of the upper half, 22, 25, 27, 30, 76, so Q3 = 27. IQR is Q3 minus Q1 = 12 minutes, the spread of the middle 50%. The standard deviation is roughly the typical distance of values from the mean: variance about 341.3, s about 18.5 minutes. Without the 76-minute value, s is about 6.1: one outlier triples the SD.

What do the data say? · Spread

The standard deviation is the typical distance from the mean

s = √( Σ(x − x)² / (n − 1) )
x
the sample mean
n
how many values; we divide by n − 1 for a sample

We divide by n minus 1, not n, for a sample; this corrects for the sample mean being closer to the data than the true mean. For the commute example the variance is about 341.3 and s is about 18.5 minutes; without the 76, s is about 6.1.

What do the data say? · Outliers

The 1.5 × IQR rule flags the 76-minute commute as an outlier

Five-number summary: minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum, here 12, 15, 21, 27, 76. A value is a suspected outlier if it is below Q1 minus 1.5 times IQR or above Q3 plus 1.5 times IQR. Here 1.5 times 12 is 18, so the fences are minus 3 and 45. The 76 lies beyond 45, so it is an outlier. A box plot draws the box from Q1 to Q3 with a line at the median, whiskers to the most extreme values that are not outliers (12 and 30), and outliers as separate dots (76).

What do the data say? · How unusual?

A z-score counts standard deviations from the mean

z = (x − mean) / SD
+ / −
above or below the mean
use
compare values on different scales

Exam: mean 70, SD 8. Score 86 → z = (86 − 70) / 8 = 2

A z-score says how many standard deviations a value is above (plus) or below (minus) the mean. The exam has mean 70 and SD 8; a score of 86 has z = 2, two SDs above the mean. z-scores let us compare values on different scales, for example a score on a hard test versus an easy one.

What do the data say? · Bell-shaped data

For bell-shaped data, 68%, 95% and 99.7% lie within 1, 2 and 3 SDs

The 68-95-99.7 rule applies to roughly normal, bell-shaped distributions: about 68% of values lie within 1 SD of the mean, about 95% within 2 SDs, about 99.7% within 3 SDs. Exam example, mean 70, SD 8: about 68% of scores between 62 and 78, about 95% between 54 and 86, about 99.7% between 46 and 94. A score of 86 is better than about 97.5% of the class. The rule does not apply to strongly skewed data like the commute times.

What do the data say? · Caution

Correlation is not causation: look for the confounder

Two variables are associated, or correlated, when they tend to move together. Across a year, ice-cream sales and drownings rise and fall together. Ice cream does not cause drowning; hot weather drives both. Hot weather is a confounding variable, a third variable that affects both variables being studied. Other reasons for a correlation without causation: reverse causation and coincidence, especially when many variables are searched. Only a randomised experiment, from earlier, can show cause and effect. Ask the room for another confounder example.

Summary

Collect well, plot first, then summarise

Data

  • Data become conclusions amid variation
  • Population → μ, σ; sample → x̄, s
  • Categorical or numerical

Collection

  • Random samples, randomised experiments
  • Bigger biased samples do not help
  • Always plot first

Describing

  • Mean vs median; range, IQR, SD
  • Outliers: 1.5 × IQR rule
  • z-scores; 68–95–99.7
  • Correlation is not causation

Third statement of the main idea: how data are collected matters most, then plot first, then summarise with centre and spread, and look for confounders before claiming causation.
