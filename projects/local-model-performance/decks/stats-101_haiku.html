STATS 101 · Module 1

Data, samples and describing data

By the end you can tell a population from a sample, choose a graph, describe a data set, find its outliers, and explain why correlation is not causation.

(About 1 minute.) Welcome. Read the promise on screen aloud: these are the module's learning objectives in plain words. Say that no statistics is needed, only high-school algebra. Set the tone: data are messy, and the job is to look at them carefully before trusting any number.

Why statistics?

Statistics is the science of collecting, describing and drawing conclusions from data.

It works in the presence of variation and uncertainty.

(About 2 minutes.) This is the main idea, first of three times we say it. Give the everyday examples from the module: opinion polls, clinical trials of new medicines, sports analytics, weather forecasts, and A/B tests on websites. Then name the two branches. Descriptive statistics summarises the data we have, with tables, graphs and averages; most of this module is descriptive. Inferential statistics uses a sample to draw conclusions about a larger population, with a measure of uncertainty; that comes in later modules.

Part 1 · Where do the data come from?

A map of deaths pointed to the cause

In 1854 John Snow marked each cholera death on a street map. The deaths clustered around one water pump on Broad Street. The pump's handle was removed.

(About 2 minutes.) This is the first of two stories. The lesson is that a simple picture of the data pointed to the cause. Snow did not start with a formula; he plotted the deaths and looked. That is the first habit of this module: plot the data before computing anything. Part 1 · Where do the data come from?

A huge biased sample is worse than a small, good one

  • 1936: about 10 million ballots mailed, about 2.4 million returned.
  • Drawn largely from telephone directories and car-registration lists.
  • It predicted Landon would beat Roosevelt. Roosevelt won about 61% of the popular vote.
  • George Gallup's sample of about 50,000 correctly predicted Roosevelt's win.

(About 2 minutes.) The Literary Digest mailed about 10 million ballots and got about 2.4 million back, and its prediction was wrong. Its list came mostly from telephone directories and car-registration lists, which left out poorer voters. Gallup used a much smaller sample, about 50,000 people, chosen better, and was right. Takeaway: how data are collected matters more than how much there is. Repeat it: a huge biased sample is worse than a small well-chosen one.

Part 1 · Vocabulary

We want to know the population; we only measure a sample

(About 2 minutes.) Three clicks: the population, then the sample, then the arrow. A population is the entire group we want to know about. A sample is the part we actually collected data from. A parameter describes the population and is usually unknown; a statistic is computed from the sample and used to estimate the parameter. Notation: population mean μ and population standard deviation σ; sample mean x̄ and sample standard deviation s. Memory aid: Population goes with Parameter, Sample goes with Statistic.

Part 1 · Sampling

Random samples spread across the population; convenience samples clump

(About 1.5 minutes.) Each grid stands for a population, and the bright dots are the sample. On the left, the sample was chosen by chance, so it is spread across the whole population. On the right, the sample is whoever was easiest to reach, so it clumps in one corner. Ask the room: which picture is more like the population? The next slide names the five sampling methods.

Part 1 · Sampling methods

Four methods use chance; convenience sampling is the one to avoid

How it worksExample

(About 2 minutes.) Go row by row. Simple random: every group of n individuals has the same chance of being chosen. Stratified: split the population into groups, called strata, and sample from each. Cluster: randomly choose whole groups and survey everyone in them. Systematic: take every k-th individual from a list after a random start. Convenience: whoever is easiest to reach. It is not random, so avoid it. The cafeteria example is the one to remember.

Part 1 · Bias

Bias: four ways a sample misleads, and size fixes none of them

  1. Selection bias

    The way the sample is chosen leaves out part of the population.

  2. Non-response bias

    The people who do not respond differ from those who do.

  3. Response bias

    Question wording or setting pushes people towards an answer.

  4. Voluntary response bias

    Only people with strong opinions choose to take part.

  5. A bigger sample does not fix bias

    It only makes a biased answer look more precise.

(About 2 minutes.) Selection bias: the Literary Digest's phone and car lists left out poorer voters in 1936. Non-response bias: the people who do not answer differ from those who do. Response bias: a question such as "Don't you agree that...?" pushes people. Voluntary response bias: online polls and call-in shows hear mainly from people with strong opinions. Fence this idea: a bigger sample does not fix bias. It only makes a biased answer look more precise.

Part 1 · Study design

Only a randomised experiment can show cause and effect

  1. Observational study

    Researchers observe without assigning treatments, e.g. surveying students about sleep and grades. It shows association, not cause and effect.

  2. Experiment

    Researchers assign the treatments and compare outcomes, with a control group, random assignment, and a placebo and blinding where possible.

(About 2 minutes.) The key difference: in an observational study the researchers only observe; in an experiment they assign treatments and compare outcomes. Good experiments use a control group, random assignment, a placebo where possible, and blinding. Double-blind means neither the subjects nor the researchers know who got which treatment. This is the second of three times we say the main idea: only a well-designed randomised experiment can establish cause and effect.

Part 2 · Types of variables

Every variable is either categorical or numerical

(About 2 minutes.) Build the tree from the top. First question: is the variable categorical, meaning it puts people into groups, or numerical, meaning it is a measurement or a count? Categorical splits into nominal, which have no order, and ordinal, which have a natural order. Numerical splits into discrete, which are counts of whole numbers, and continuous, which are measurements on a scale. Ask the room for one example of each before revealing the examples. Stress the watch-out: a student ID is a number but it is categorical.

Part 2 · Levels of measurement

(About 2 minutes.) Walk across the columns. Nominal: categories with no order, such as eye colour. Ordinal: order, but the gaps are not equal, such as race finishing position. Interval: order and equal intervals, but no true zero, such as temperature in degrees Celsius. Ratio: all three, such as weight, income or time. The next slide shows why the true zero matters.

Why it matters

20 °C is not twice as hot as 10 °C. 20 kg is twice 10 kg.

Interval data have no true zero; ratio data do.

(About 1 minute.) This is the reason the levels matter. Temperature in degrees Celsius is an interval scale: zero degrees is not the absence of temperature, so 20 °C is not twice as hot as 10 °C. Weight is a ratio scale: zero kilograms is no weight at all, so 20 kg really is twice 10 kg. Before you say that a value is twice another, check the level of measurement.

Part 3 · Shape and centre

Right skew pulls the mean above the median

(About 2 minutes.) Every numerical distribution is described by its shape, centre, spread and unusual features. The left curve is roughly symmetric, so the mean and median coincide. The right curve has a long tail to the right, as incomes do; the tail pulls the mean up above the median. These curves are illustrations of shape only, with no data behind them.

Part 3 · Centre

The mean shares out the total; the median takes the middle

x̄ = Σx / n
Σx

n
how many values
Median

Mode

(About 2 minutes.) The mean is x̄ = Σx / n: add the values and divide by how many there are. The median is the middle value of the ordered data, and when n is even it is the average of the two middle values. The mode is the most frequent value. Next we apply all three to ten real commute times.

Worked example · commute times

Ten commutes give three different centres

26minmean: 260 ÷ 10
21minmedian: average of 20 and 22
15minmode: appears twice

Commute times in minutes, 10 students: 12, 15, 15, 18, 20, 22, 25, 27, 30, 76

(About 2 minutes.) Work it on the board with the class. The values sum to 260, so the mean is 26 minutes. Sorted, the two middle values are 20 and 22, so the median is 21. The mode is 15, the only value that appears twice. Point out the 76: it is the reason the mean sits above most of the commutes.

Part 3 · Outliers and centre

One 76-minute commute moves the mean, but barely the median

All ten commutesWithout the 76
Mean26 minabout 20.4 min

(About 1.5 minutes.) Remove the 76-minute commute and the mean drops from 26 to about 20.4, while the median only drops from 21 to 20. The median is resistant to outliers; the mean is not. In a right-skewed distribution the mean is pulled above the median, and for skewed data such as house prices or incomes, use the median.

Part 3 · Spread

The outlier stretches the range; the IQR covers the middle 50%

64minrange: 76 − 12
12minIQR: Q3 − Q1 = 27 − 15
18.5minstandard deviation, s

Quartiles: lower half 12, 15, 15, 18, 20 gives Q1 = 15; upper half 22, 25, 27, 30, 76 gives Q3 = 27

(About 2 minutes.) Range is the maximum minus the minimum: 76 minus 12, which is 64 minutes, and very sensitive to the outlier. Q1 is the median of the lower half, 15; Q3 is the median of the upper half, 27. The IQR, Q3 minus Q1, is 12 minutes and is the spread of the middle 50%. Standard deviation is roughly the typical distance of values from the mean: about 18.5 minutes here.

Part 3 · Standard deviation

One 76-minute outlier triples the standard deviation

s = √( Σ(x − x̄)² / (n − 1) )
x̄
the sample mean
n − 1
for a sample we divide by n − 1, not n; this corrects for the sample mean sitting closer to the data than the true mean

With the 76: s ≈ 18.5 min. Without it: s ≈ 6.1 min.

(About 2 minutes.) Read the formula in words: for each value, take its distance from the mean and square it, add those up, divide by n minus 1, then take the square root. The variance, before the square root, is about 341.3 for the ten commutes, so s is about 18.5 minutes. Without the 76 the standard deviation is about 6.1 minutes: one outlier roughly triples it. This is the same lesson as the mean and range.

Part 3 · Outliers

The 1.5 × IQR rule flags the 76-minute commute

(About 2.5 minutes.) The five-number summary is minimum 12, Q1 15, median 21, Q3 27 and maximum 76. Draw the box from Q1 to Q3 with a line at the median. The whiskers run to the most extreme values that are not outliers, 12 and 30. The 1.5 × IQR rule: with IQR 12, 1.5 × 12 = 18, so the fences are 15 − 18 = −3 and 27 + 18 = 45. The 76 lies beyond 45, so it is a suspected outlier. Click through the builds slowly.

Part 3 · z-scores

A z-score says how many SDs a value sits from the mean

z = (x − mean) / SD
x
the value we are looking at
SD
the standard deviation of the data

Exam: mean 70, SD 8. A score of 86 has z = (86 − 70) / 8 = 2: two SDs above the mean.

(About 2 minutes.) A z-score is the number of standard deviations a value lies above (positive) or below (negative) the mean. Worked example: an exam with mean 70 and SD 8. A score of 86 has z = 2. z-scores also let us compare values on different scales, such as a score on a hard test and a score on an easy one.

Part 3 · Bell-shaped data

For bell-shaped data, about 68%, 95% and 99.7% lie within 1, 2 and 3 SDs

(About 2.5 minutes.) Build the bell, then the three brackets from the inside out. About 68% of values lie within 1 SD of the mean, about 95% within 2 SDs and about 99.7% within 3 SDs. In the exam, that means scores of 62 to 78, 54 to 86 and 46 to 94. A score of 86 (z = 2) is better than about 97.5% of the class. Warn them: the rule does not apply to strongly skewed data like the commute times.

Part 3 · Correlation

Ice cream does not cause drownings; hot weather drives both

(About 2.5 minutes.) Ice-cream sales and drownings rise and fall together across a year, so they are correlated. Ice cream does not cause drowning. Hot weather drives both, and it is a confounding variable: a third variable that affects both things being studied. Other reasons for a correlation without causation are reverse causation and coincidence. The only way to show cause and effect is a randomised experiment. This is the third time we say the main idea, and the fence: correlation is not causation.

Summary

Collect carefully, plot first, then describe with centre and spread

  1. Design matters more than size

    Random sampling and randomised experiments. A bigger biased sample does not help.

  2. Know the variable

    Categorical (nominal, ordinal) or numerical (discrete, continuous).

  3. Plot before you compute

    Anscombe's quartet: the same summary numbers, four different pictures.

  4. Centre and spread

    Mean (sensitive) or median (resistant); range, IQR, SD; outliers beyond 1.5 × IQR.

  5. Correlation is not causation

    Look for confounders. Next module: probability, the language of uncertainty.

(About 1 minute.) This is the third and final time we say the main idea: collect carefully, plot first, then describe with centre and spread. Walk the five rows quickly.
